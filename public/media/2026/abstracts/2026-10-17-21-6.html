Abstract

Enteric oxalate nephropathy (ON) as the underlying cause of acute on chronic renal failure requiring dialysis in renal recipients is rarely reported and may be misdiagnosed, leading to inappropriate management. We described a 65-year-old diabetic uremic man who had received cadaveric kidney transplantation with CKD, stage IIIB (serum creatinine 1.8-2.0 mg/dl) for 9 years on optimal immunosuppressants presented with progressive leg edema, body weight gain, and exertional dyspnea following intermittent water diarrhea for 2 weeks. Physical examination revealed higher blood pressure 150/100 mmHg, engorged jugular vein, and generalized edema. His relevant laboratory findings showed a declined hemoglobin from 10.3 to 9.8 mg/dl, serum creatinine rose from 3.7 to 7.4 mg/dl, hyperkalemia (5.9 mmol/L) and hyperchlormic metabolic acidosis, severe hypocalcemia (6.0 mg/dl), higher serum CRP 3.6 mg/dl, and no RBC or WBC in the urine, but mild proteinuria. His renal function is not improved with volume repletion or alkalizastion and one dose of pulse steroid therapy for presumptive diagnosis of acute kidney rejection. After emergent hemodialysis to control his uremic symptoms, he received renal biopsy typically showing numerous oxalate deposition in the intratubular lumen consistent with ON. His urine oxalate excretion was still much higher after the diagnosis of ON and become normal one month later. With aggressive control of watery diarrhea, low oxalate diet, high calcium supplementation, tight control of sugar and reduced immunosuppressants, his renal function (serum creatinine 4.5-5.0 mg/dl) was improved without the need of dialysis. This case highlights the importance of considering ON as a potential cause of acute kidney injury in renal recipient with diarrhea. Early determination of urine oxalate excretion may be needed.
